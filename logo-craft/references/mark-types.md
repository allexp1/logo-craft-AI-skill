# Mark types: choosing the kind of logo

The categories overlap, and most identities need more than one (a symbol for the icon, a wordmark for the header, a lockup for everything else). Choose by communication need and by where the mark must live, not by taste.

Contents: 1 the taxonomy · 2 decision guide · 3 what the name's letters offer · 4 the compact symbol and the app icon · 5 responsive and dynamic systems · 6 pitfalls by type

## 1. The taxonomy

| Type | What it is | Choose when | Watch out for | Reference examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Wordmark (logotype)** | The name set or drawn as the mark | The name is short and distinctive, or must be taught (new company); the brand lives in text-heavy places | Needs a separate compact symbol for icons; fails at 16 px; generic if set in an untouched geometric sans | Google, FedEx, Coca-Cola, Burberry 2023 |
| **Lettermark / monogram** | One to three initials as a mark | The name is long, or a short acronym is the real name; the initials have interesting shapes | Initials are a crowded space (every A, M, S monogram exists); needs the name alongside until known | IBM, HBO, CNN, GE |
| **Pictorial** | A recognisable thing, simplified | The thing has a distinctive silhouette and is not the category's cliché | Literalism; the thing dates; often already taken | Apple, Twitter's bird, Shell, Target |
| **Abstract** | Geometry with no literal referent | The offer is broad or will change; the company can commit to teaching it | Needs time and consistency to acquire meaning; easy to make generic | Chase, Nike, Mastercard, Pepsi |
| **Emblem / badge** | Name inside a contained shape (seal, shield, circle, crest) | Heritage, institutions, teams, beverages; the mark must work as a sticker or patch | Small sizes: an emblem needs a compact alternative; text inside dies at 32 px | Starbucks, BMW, Harley-Davidson, universities |
| **Mascot / character** | A figure with personality | Kids, food, sports, anything built on friendliness and story | Expensive to keep consistent; needs a simplified head or silhouette for icons | Michelin, KFC, Duolingo's owl |
| **Combination** | Symbol plus wordmark, designed together | Almost every new product: teaches name and symbol together and lets them separate later | Both parts must hold alone; the lockup rules must be written | Mastercard, Slack, Adidas |
| **Negative-space device** | A second reading in the counter or the space between parts | A gift found later; must never be the only reading | Legibility sacrificed for the trick | FedEx arrow, NBC peacock, Toblerone bear |
| **Dynamic identity** | A system of controlled variation (a grammar, not one shape) | Institutions, culture, events, platforms with many sub-brands | Needs a stable anchor (usually the wordmark) or nothing is recognised | MIT Media Lab, London 2012, Google Doodles on a fixed wordmark |
| **Responsive identity** | One mark in several levels of detail for several sizes | Every digital brand, in practice | Each level must feel like the same mark; write the switch sizes down | Google's wordmark, G and dots |

## 2. Decision guide

Answer these in order; the answers usually leave one or two types standing.

1. **Where is the smallest important place?** App icon or favicon: you need a symbol or monogram regardless of what else you make. Website header only: a wordmark may be enough, with a favicon letter.
2. **How long is the name?** Up to about seven letters: a wordmark can be the primary mark. Longer: lead with a symbol or monogram, the wordmark is secondary. Two words: consider a stacked lockup from the start.
3. **Is the name descriptive or invented?** Invented names ("Kixplo") need teaching: combination mark, wordmark prominent. Descriptive names ("Invoice Ninja") already explain, so the symbol can be abstract.
4. **Will the offer change?** Startups pivot; a literal pictorial of today's feature ages fast. Prefer abstract or letter-based.
5. **What is the category convention, and do you want recognition or difference?** A bank that looks like a bank (emblem, serif, blue) buys trust; a neobank that looks like a bank loses its reason to exist. Decide, then write it down.
6. **Does the audience read right to left or in several scripts?** Then the symbol carries more weight (it is script-neutral) and the wordmark exists per language.
7. **Is there a story worth a character?** Only for kids, food, entertainment and community brands with a budget to keep the character alive.

## 3. What the name's letters offer

Before inventing a symbol, read the name as shapes:

- **Symmetric letters** (A, H, I, M, O, T, U, V, W, X, Y; o, v, w, x) make stable monograms and can be mirrored into symbols.
- **Round letters** (O, C, G, Q, o, c, e) become lenses, rings, planets, apertures, speech bubbles; the counter is a free second element.
- **Diagonals** (A, K, V, W, X, Z, k, v, w, x, z) bring motion and can hide an arrow or a check.
- **Repeated letters** (the two o's in Google, the two l's in Kellogg's, Hebrew doubled letters) suggest rhythm or a pair motif.
- **Ascenders and descenders** (b, d, f, h, k, l; g, j, p, q, y) set the wordmark's silhouette. A name with none (e.g. "nexus") is a flat ribbon and needs a strong x-height treatment or caps.
- **Ligatures and connections**: two letters that can share a stroke (ff, ll, rt, Th, the Hebrew ל reaching over a neighbour) make an ownable detail. Keep the gap that distinguishes letters (the Kia lesson).
- **The first letter** is the default monogram and favicon; check that it is not the same first letter and shape family as the nearest competitor.

## 4. The compact symbol and the app icon

The symbol you design becomes the app icon on a coloured square (iOS: rounded square mask applied by the system, Icon Composer layers; Android: any mask, 66 dp safe zone of a 108 dp canvas; PWA maskable: a central circle of 40% radius). Design the symbol so that:

- it fills roughly 55 to 65% of the square's side (export kit default 0.62) and still reads inside a circle;
- it has no detail that depends on the square's corners;
- it works as a single-colour glyph for Android themed (monochrome) icons and iOS tinted icons;
- a light and a dark version exist (the dark version usually lightens the symbol or swaps symbol and background).

If the symbol is a wordmark's first letter, draw it as its own object with its own optical corrections; do not crop the wordmark.

## 5. Responsive and dynamic systems

A responsive mark has levels: full lockup (header, documents), symbol plus short name (narrow headers), symbol alone (icons), and a compact glyph (16 px, embroidery). Design the levels together so each is clearly the same mark: the same silhouette idea, the same angle family, the same colour. Write down the size at which each level takes over (for example: lockup above 120 px wide, symbol from 32 to 120 px, compact glyph below 32 px).

A dynamic identity needs a grammar (what varies: colour, fill pattern, the position of one element) and an anchor (what never varies). Without the anchor there is no identity, only a style.

## 6. Pitfalls by type

| Type | Common failure | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Wordmark | Untouched font, no ownable detail | Modify one or two letters with intent (a terminal, a join, a counter); unify widths; kern optically |
| Monogram | Letters forced together until unreadable | Keep the distinguishing gap; test unprompted reading |
| Pictorial | The category cliché (tooth, house, leaf, camera) | Go one step more abstract or pick a different referent from the word map |
| Abstract | Generic geometry (three arcs, a spiral) | Add one specific property: a particular angle, a cut, a proportion that is not 1:1 |
| Emblem | Text dies at small sizes | Design the badge with a compact version (symbol only) and switch at a written size |
| Mascot | Inconsistent redraws | Define the head silhouette as the symbol; keep a construction sheet |
| Negative space | The trick is the only reading | Make the primary reading work first; treat the second reading as a bonus |
| Dynamic | Everything varies | Fix the wordmark or one element; vary one dimension only |
