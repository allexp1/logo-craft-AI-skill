# Case studies and transferable lessons

The facts below are from the companies' or designers' own accounts (see `sources.md`); the lessons are interpretations. Take the *principle* from each case, never the shape. Every mark here is a registered trademark of its owner.

Contents: 1 marks that work and why · 2 redesigns that kept the equity · 3 redesigns that failed and why · 4 negative space done right · 5 systems and responsive identities · 6 scripts and lettering · 7 a one-page summary of lessons

## 1. Marks that work and why

| Mark | Facts | Lesson for your work |
|---|---|---|
| **Nike swoosh** (Carolyn Davidson, 1971) | Paid 35 dollars; Phil Knight reportedly said "I don't love it, but it will grow on me" | Immediate affection is not the test; a single confident gesture plus years of consistent use is. Do not kill a strong simple form because the first reaction is lukewarm |
| **Apple** (Rob Janoff, 1977) | Drawn as a silhouette; the bite was added so the fruit would not be mistaken for a cherry (scale cue). The "golden ratio construction" is a later myth | Solve recognition and scale cues before symbolism. Never retrofit a sacred-geometry story |
| **IBM eight-bar** (Paul Rand, 1972) | Stripes unify the three letters and suggest speed and precision; IBM publishes distinct positive and reversed constructions | A module (the stripe) can carry the idea; production variants (reversed) are part of the design, not an afterthought |
| **ABC** (Rand, 1962) | Lowercase letters in a circle, elemental geometry | A tiny vocabulary of forms yields a durable whole; sixty years of use with minor tuning |
| **Chase octagon** (Chermayeff & Geismar, 1961) | Abstract, initially resisted by executives; one of the first abstract corporate marks in the US | Abstraction needs commitment and repetition; it then outlives every product line |
| **Mastercard circles** | Two overlapping circles since 1968; the 2016 Pentagram redesign removed the stripes, kept the circles, and in 2019 dropped the name from the symbol | Identify the recognition asset and protect it; simplify everything else |
| **Airbnb Bélo** (DesignStudio, 2014) | Built around "belonging" after immersive research; replaced a literal bubble-letter wordmark | A broader organisational idea outlasts a literal depiction of the current product (rooms) |
| **Slack** (Pentagram, 2019) | The eleven-colour hashtag broke at small sizes and on colour backgrounds; the new octothorpe uses four colours and two simple shapes | Operational reliability (small sizes, backgrounds, legibility) is a design criterion equal to familiarity |
| **FedEx** (Lindon Leader, Landor, 1994) | Arrow between E and x; the wordmark is clean whether or not the arrow is seen; the colour of "Ex" changes per division | Typography and negative space can carry the concept together; the primary reading must work first |
| **Target** | A bullseye: the name as the mark | When the name is a thing with a strong silhouette, depict it and stop |

## 2. Redesigns that kept the equity

- **Mastercard 2016**: kept circles and colours, dropped stripes and later the name; readable at favicon size.
- **Pepsi 2023**: reunited the name inside the globe, added black for contrast, built a "pulse" motion system; heritage and motion coexist.
- **Burberry 2023**: brought back the Equestrian Knight and serif lettering it had dropped in the 2018 sans-serif "blanding" redesign; character can be recovered from the archive.
- **Google 2015**: the wordmark became a geometric sans, but the four colours and their order stayed; the compact G and the dots extended the system.
- **Nokia 2023**: repositioned toward B2B technology with a new wordmark; judge it against the business intent, not nostalgia.
- **Instagram 2013 and 2016**: 2013 refined the script's joins (Mackey Saturday); 2016 replaced the skeuomorphic camera with a flat glyph and a gradient but kept the camera's silhouette and the rainbow's warmth.

## 3. Redesigns that failed, and why

| Case | What happened | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| **Gap 2010** | A new Helvetica wordmark with a gradient square replaced the blue box; reverted within a week after public backlash | Establish the problem and the equity at risk before replacing a mark; a redesign without a reason is a loss |
| **Tropicana 2009** | New packaging dropped the orange-with-straw image; sales reportedly fell about 20% and the old design returned | Test shelf recognition of the whole package, not the logo in isolation; recognisable imagery is equity |
| **Kia 2021** | A connected stylised wordmark that many read as "KN"; searches for "KN car" rose | Run unprompted reading tests; explanation does not fix a first-read failure |
| **Jaguar 2024** | Mixed-case device mark and a repositioning campaign drew heavy criticism | Attention is neither success nor failure; judge against the strategic intent and later recognition, and do not confuse controversy with distinctiveness |
| **London 2012** (Wolff Olins, 2007) | A fragmented "2012" shape divided opinion; the flexible identity worked across the event's applications | Judge a system by its whole application set, not by one static image; still, the static mark must stand on its own because that is what the public sees first |
| **UPS 2003** | Rand's 1961 tied parcel was removed as UPS broadened beyond parcels; the glossy 3D shield dated quickly and was flattened in 2014 | Separate the strategic reason (scope changed) from the aesthetic fashion (gloss); keep the structure, drop the finish |

## 4. Negative space done right

- **FedEx**: the arrow lives in the space between E and x. Primary reading intact.
- **NBC peacock** (1986 version, Chermayeff & Geismar): the bird's body is the white space between six feathers.
- **Toblerone**: a bear stands in the mountain's white; it is a secret, not a requirement.
- **Baskin-Robbins**: the "31" is formed by differently coloured parts of the B and R.
- **Amazon** is a counter-example in the sense that the smile-arrow is a *positive* element; not every "clever" logo is negative space.

Rule, restated: the first reading first, the hidden reading as a gift.

## 5. Systems and responsive identities

- **Google 2015**: wordmark for wide spaces, the G for icons, four dots for motion and listening states. The levels are designed together and share colour and geometry.
- **MIT Media Lab** (Pentagram, 2014): a fixed grid generates a monogram for each research group; the anchor is the grid and the letterforms.
- **Mastercard**: the symbol alone from 2019; the system defined when the name appears.
- **OpenAI 2025**: a refined "blossom" symbol, a custom typeface (OpenAI Sans) and usage architecture; the symbol change is the smallest part of the system.

## 7. The lessons on one page

1. Solve recognition first; meaning comes from use.
2. Protect the recognition asset in a redesign; change everything else freely.
3. Small-size reliability is a design criterion, not a production detail (Slack).
4. Test unprompted reading (Kia) and shelf recognition (Tropicana).
5. A redesign needs a strategic sentence; without it, do not redesign (Gap).
6. The primary reading must work before the hidden one (FedEx).
7. Fashionable finishes date; structures last (UPS 2003 to 2014).
8. Design the system, not a picture (Google, Mastercard, OpenAI).
9. Character is equity too; reduction can go too far (Burberry).
10. Controversy is not a result either way (Jaguar); wait for recognition data.
